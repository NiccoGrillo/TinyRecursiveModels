# What CRAM changes for learned residual reasoning

20 November 2026 · Research note accompanying the [living notebook](notebook/project_notebook.pdf).

**Decision.** Keep the rule-free model. Give an ordinary search algorithm the same learned score and a competitive set of moves. The primary question is whether learned residual mixing repairs genuine mistakes more efficiently than this search or a cheaper neural corrector. A second question, conditional on an advantage for persistent memory, is whether carrying that memory saves the work of recomputing a useful intermediate quantity.

## Reading the evidence

Hoover, Blayney and Whittington train CRAM, a classical categorical energy. On a discrete board its energy sums unary costs and one table entry for each unordered pair of cells. One-cell pseudolikelihood (PL) asks how probable the demonstrated value is when all other output cells are supplied correctly. Every term that does not touch the edited cell cancels in its conditional. A single learned energy can thus fit local comparisons without solving training puzzles. At deployment, an explicit algorithm searches that energy. The continuous mean-field dynamics have a descent result at fixed parameters and temperature. Actual Sudoku inference uses positive-temperature stochastic moves that can increase energy. The learned pair costs are parameters of a scalar energy. Our matrix W instead mixes a vector of residuals while reasoning. [Paper §§2–3 and Appendix E](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.17309v2)

## A local objective can be right for the wrong reason

Let $n_d(y)$ count occurrences of digit $d$ on a completed board and set

$$
E_{\mathrm{count}}(y)=\sum_{d=1}^9 {n_d(y)\choose 2}.
$$

Every valid Sudoku has nine copies of every digit. Replacing one digit changes two counts from $(9,9)$ to $(8,10)$, increasing energy by exactly one. In the one-cell conditional from the scaled energy $\lambda E_{\mathrm{count}}$, the true digit has probability $1/(1+8e^{-\lambda})$. This holds at every free cell of every valid Sudoku, even on unseen puzzles. Yet every balanced board has the same energy. Swapping two different free digits can invalidate a correct board with no score change. Every such balanced invalid board is itself a strict one-cell local minimum. An evaluation based only on fresh puzzles and fresh one-cell edits would still reward this shortcut.

This example belongs to CRAM's own pairwise model class. It uses a dense equal-digit interaction between every pair. Sparse regularization can distinguish it from the actual Sudoku graph. Consequently the ambiguity is a property of the one-edit supervision in isolation. We checked what the released model actually learned.

### An independent check on the three released checkpoints

We inspected the full-precision, uncompressed W and B tensors in the authors' release. The positive equal-digit penalty graph coincides exactly with the 810 Sudoku peer pairs in all three seeds. There are also small *negative* equal-digit entries outside that graph, so the article's literal description of all remaining costs as zero is too strong for these files. The smaller entries do not overturn the following local results. The authors' stopping thresholds, about 0.00083–0.000997, lie strictly between the upper bound on valid grids and the lower bound on invalid ones. **For all three models, thresholding the exact energy of a completed grid with clues clamped recognizes precisely the valid completions.** This holds for arbitrary clue patterns, including puzzles with several completions. **Proof of global separation.** Write $E=P+R$, where $P$ contains the positive equal-digit peer costs, each at least $m>0$. Every invalid completed grid has at least two *distinct* peer pairs with equal digits. To see why, suppose exactly one such pair existed. Its two cells differ in row or column, so at least one of the row and column partitions has no duplicated digit anywhere. Each digit therefore occurs nine times on the whole board. In a unit containing the offending pair, one digit is missing. In another unit of the same partition that missing digit must occur more than once. This gives a second pair. Hence $P=0$ for a valid grid and $P\ge2m$ for an invalid one.

Write $R_{ij}(a,b)$ for a remaining pair table oriented with $i$ first, and $B_i(a)$ for a retained bias. Give each endpoint half the value of its edge. Relax consistency among the neighboring digit assignments to obtain

$$
L=\sum_i\min_a\left[B_i(a)+\tfrac12\sum_{j\ne i}\min_b R_{ij}(a,b)\right]\le R(y).
$$

Replacing both minima with maxima gives an upper bound $U$. The audit computes $U=0$ for each checkpoint. Every invalid board has energy at least $2m+L$, which gives the fourth column. All sums and extrema in this calculation use the exact dyadic rational values of the stored coefficients, via integer arithmetic. An independent fraction-arithmetic calculation gave the same three values. The rule used to prove this result is external analysis of frozen published weights. The proposed general-purpose model receives no Sudoku graph or solver.

**Stronger margins near a solution.** For each position $i$ bound the maximum change of the remaining energy under any single edit by

$$
D_i=\max_{a,b}\left(|B_i(b)-B_i(a)|+\sum_{j\ne i}\max_v |R_{ij}(b,v)-R_{ij}(a,v)|\right).
$$

The remainder changes by at most $D_i$ for a replacement and $D_i+D_j$ for a swap, by telescoping through the intermediate board. Both edits make a valid grid invalid. For a swap in different rows, each affected row gets a duplicate. In the same row, each affected column gets a duplicate. Subtract the largest one or two distinct-position bounds from $2m$ to obtain the last two columns. A puzzle-preserving move edits only free cells.

To reproduce, point [the CPU audit script](../scripts/audit_cram_sudoku.py) at the three seed model.pt files under checkpoints/sudoku-extreme in the pinned CRAM checkout. It prints the tensor-file SHA-256 and margins for each file. No puzzle data, training, search or GPU is required.

## The experiment the critic retained

1. **Learn the field.** Keep the current local calibration of geometric residuals near correct answer embeddings, the absolute signed-score anchor and the rollout task loss. Compare the existing known-negative score training, an auxiliary one-edit conditional score loss, and substitution of composition-preserving negatives for part of the known-negative budget. Match the number of scored alternatives and training compute. Include matched *two*-edit random negatives to distinguish preserved composition from edit distance. Use unlocked positions and multiple examples per original input. For multiple valid outputs, a candidate gets an invalid label only if its invalidity is known. A demonstrated conditional distribution can differ from the set of all valid answers.
2. **Make natural recovery the primary mechanism test.** Freeze a common reference solver and fork states at predeclared steps on held-out original inputs. Save its untouched wrong states, their decoded candidates, and a separate correct cohort. Within each frozen field give every latent controller identical starting states. Across fields, keep the codebook, initializer, puzzle–step pairs and source distribution fixed. Give score-only discrete search the same decoded candidate, using the fixed codebook to score its edits. It has a different representation of a starting state, so its comparison assesses the whole inference procedure. Single-site changes plus budgeted two-site changes or swaps, an uphill-move policy and restarts all need comparable tuning. Proposals never see error locations or the reference answer at inference. For output representations where swaps have no meaningful support, use independently validated errors instead.
3. **Attribute the gain.** Compare solve rate from ordinary initialization and repair from common wrong states across measured cost, alongside correct-state retention. Report paired differences by original input, uncertainty, candidate-score calls, latent derivatives, restarts, wall-clock time and incorrect stops. Report fixed-budget outcomes first. Strong score-only search and the cheap neural proposals $q-sH$ and $q-Gc$ are the main alternatives. Comparing diagonal, stateless full and persistent full mixing on the same continuous representation isolates the added components. Repeat the strongest comparison under joint training before making an architectural claim. Better scores on synthetic swaps alone support a better discriminator. Real recovery gains shared by all solvers indicate a better field. Only a residual advantage over the tuned competitors at matched cost motivates the more expensive controller.
4. **Investigate memory only if it pays.** In Maze-Hard, CRAM computes $(mI-A)z=e_G$ on the known open-cell graph before decoding, where $A$ is adjacency, $m>4$, and $e_G$ injects current at the goal. A generic control for our model would recompute an auxiliary state from the present $(q,x)$, with no supplied graph or goal semantics. First compare a trained reset-at-each-step controller. If persistent memory still leads, train one update $H(q,x,u)$ contractive in $u$ at fixed $(q,x)$, exposing it to both warm and cold starts. At the same candidate and same $k$ inner calls, measure the difference between the resulting corrections and repair rates. With contraction factor $\rho<1$, warm and cold auxiliary states differ by at most $\rho^k$ times their initial separation. Check residual tolerance and pay for all iterations. A cold solve that matches warm decisions at extra cost supports a computational role for memory. It does not establish path dependence in the unrestricted model.

Discrete edit scores alone cannot train the full proposal. A smooth perturbation supported away from the supervised codewords and calibrated neighborhoods can leave every such score and local constraint unchanged and alter the derivative on a wrong trajectory. Also, a score-only loss bypasses W altogether. Whether the resulting continuous update $-J_c^\top Wc$ points toward repair must be tested on actual trajectories. The older attracting-answer-set proposal also has a distinct target. Several isolated correct solutions, a manifold of latent states decoding to one answer, and a basin of initializations that reaches a correct answer describe different phenomena.

**A concrete transfer test.** Line transcription from noisy images is one candidate outside puzzles. All arms would receive the same image encoder and initial transcription. Compare against that recognizer's native beam decoder at equal cost. Report whole-line exact match and character error rate on unmodified held-out images, including insertions and deletions. Adjacent transpositions give an auxiliary order-sensitive diagnostic on unambiguous transcripts. They do not determine the error cohort or supply the reference at test time. This is a proposed domain, with no dataset selection or performance claim yet.

**Review outcome.** Three design rounds and a fourth checkpoint-proof check supported the comparison and the certificate on the released Sudoku energies. The critic rejected a benchmark-accuracy-only architecture claim, training the main model with supplied puzzle rules, and treating success on artificial swaps as evidence of better reasoning. No v3 model was trained in this review.
